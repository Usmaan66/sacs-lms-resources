### 1.1 Diversity in the Living World
- **Biology** is the science that studies life forms and living processes. The living world shows an amazing diversity of organisms.
- Early humans could easily differentiate between non-living and living organisms. They even deified some inanimate matter (like wind, sea, fire) and some animals and plants, feeling awe or fear towards them.
- The systematic description of life forms led to detailed systems of **identification**, **nomenclature**, and **classification**.
- This also revealed similarities among living organisms, leading to cultural movements for **biodiversity conservation**.
- The range of living types is amazing, found in diverse habitats like cold mountains, forests, oceans, fresh water lakes, deserts, or hot springs.
- The beauty and complexity of living organisms make us question "what is life?". As scientists, we focus on understanding "what is living?" as opposed to non-living.
- We are surrounded by a large variety of living organisms, some visible to the naked eye and many not. Increasing the observation area increases the range and variety of organisms seen.
- Each different kind of plant, animal, or organism represents a **species**. The number of known and described species ranges between 1.7 to 1.8 million. This number refers to **biodiversity**, which is the number and types of organisms present on Earth.
- New organisms are continuously being identified as we explore new and old areas.
- Local names for plants and animals vary from place to place, even within a country, causing confusion.
- Therefore, there is a need to standardize the naming of living organisms so that a particular organism is known by the same name worldwide. This process is called **nomenclature**.
- **Nomenclature** or naming is only possible when the organism is correctly described, and we know to which organism the name is attached. This correct description and association of a name is called **identification**.
- Scientists have established procedures to assign scientific names to each known organism, which are acceptable globally.
- For plants, scientific names are based on principles and criteria provided in the **International Code for Botanical Nomenclature (ICBN)**. For animals, the **International Code of Zoological Nomenclature (ICZN)** has been evolved by animal taxonomists.
- Scientific names ensure that each organism has only one name and that the description enables anyone to arrive at the same name. They also ensure that a name has not been used for any other known organism.
- Biologists follow universally accepted principles for scientific naming. Each scientific name has two components: the **Generic name** and the **specific epithet**.
- This system of providing a name with two components is called **Binomial nomenclature**. This system was given by **Carolus Linnaeus** and is practiced worldwide. The two-word format of this naming system was found to be convenient.
- Example: The scientific name of mango is *Mangifera indica*.
    - *Mangifera* represents the **genus**.
    - *indica* is the **specific epithet** (a particular species).
