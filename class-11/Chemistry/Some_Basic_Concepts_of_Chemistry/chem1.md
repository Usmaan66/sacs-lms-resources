# UNIT 1 SOME BASIC CONCEPTS OF CHEMISTRY

## DEVELOPMENT OF CHEMISTRY

-   Chemistry, as we know it today, is a relatively modern field.
-   Historically, chemistry grew from a quest for two main things:
    -   The **Philosopher's stone** (Paras): Believed to turn basic metals like iron and copper into gold.
    -   The **'Elixir of life'**: Thought to grant immortality.
-   Ancient India had significant knowledge of scientific phenomena long before modern science.
-   Chemistry developed in India mainly as **Alchemy** and **Iatrochemistry** between 1300-1600 CE.
-   Modern chemistry began to take shape in the $18^{th}$ century in Europe, influenced by Arab alchemical traditions.
-   In ancient India, chemistry was known by various names: **Rasayan Shastra**, **Rastantra**, **Ras Kriya**, or **Rasvidya**.
-   This included knowledge of:
    -   Metallurgy
    -   Medicine
    -   Manufacture of cosmetics, glass, and dyes
-   **Archaeological findings** in Mohenjodaro and Harappa show:
    -   Use of baked bricks in construction.
    -   Mass production of pottery (regarded as an early chemical process involving mixing, molding, and heating materials).
    -   Remains of glazed pottery and gypsum cement (containing lime, sand, and $CaCO_3$).
    -   Harappans made **faience** (a type of glass for ornaments).
    -   Melting and forging of lead, silver, gold, and copper.
    -   Hardening of copper using tin and arsenic for artifacts.
    -   Glass objects found in Maski (South India, 1000-900 BCE) and Hastinapur/Taxila (North India, 1000-200 BCE).
    -   Glass and glazes colored using metal oxides.
-   **Copper metallurgy** in India dates back to chalcolithic cultures. Evidence suggests indigenous development of copper and iron extraction technologies.
-   **Rigveda** mentions tanning of leather and dyeing of cotton (1000-400 BCE).
-   **Kautilya's Arthashastra** describes salt production from the sea.
-   **Sushruta Samhita** highlights the importance of Alkalies.
-   **Charaka Samhita** mentions knowledge of preparing:
    -   Sulphuric acid, nitric acid
    -   Oxides of copper, tin, zinc
    -   Sulphates of copper, zinc, iron
    -   Carbonates of lead, iron
-   **Rasopanishada** describes gunpowder mixture preparation. Tamil texts detail fireworks using sulfur, charcoal, saltpetre (potassium nitrate), mercury, camphor, etc.
-   **Nagarjuna** (Indian scientist, chemist, alchemist, metallurgist):
    -   His work **Rasratnakar** focuses on mercury compounds.
    -   Discussed methods for extracting gold, silver, tin, and copper.
-   The book **Rsarnavam** (around 800 CE) discusses various furnaces, ovens, crucibles, and methods to identify metals by flame color.
-   **Chakrapani** discovered mercury sulfide and is credited with inventing soap using mustard oil and alkalies. Indians started making soaps in the $18^{th}$ century CE.
-   **Ajanta and Ellora paintings** show advanced ancient Indian science.
-   **Varähmihir's Brihat Samhita** (6th century CE) is an encyclopedia mentioning preparation of glutinous material for walls and roofs from plant extracts.
-   Ancient texts like **Atharvaveda** (1000 BCE) mention dyes from turmeric, madder, sunflower, etc.
-   Brihat Samhita also includes recipes for perfumes, cosmetics, and hair dyeing from plants (indigo) and minerals.
-   **Gandhayukli** describes recipes for scents, mouth perfumes, bath powders, etc.
-   Paper was known in India by the $17^{th}$ century (Chinese traveler I-tsing).
-   Ink was used in India from the $4^{th}$ century (Taxila excavations), with colors from chalk, red lead.
-   The process of **fermentation** was well-known (Vedas, Kautilya's Arthashastra mention liquors; Charaka Samhita mentions ingredients for Asavas).
-   **Atomic Theory in Ancient India**:
    -   The concept of matter being made of indivisible building blocks appeared centuries BCE.
    -   **Acharya Kanda** (Kashyap, 600 BCE) was the first proponent of **atomic theory**.
    -   He theorized very small, indivisible particles called **'Paramānu'** (atoms) that are eternal, indestructible, spherical, and in motion.
    -   He explained that these entities cannot be sensed by human organs and that different classes of substances have different varieties of atoms.
    -   Paramānu could form pairs or triplets, interacting through unseen forces. This was conceptualized about 2500 years before John Dalton.
-   **Charaka Samhita** also discusses the reduction of particle size of metals, which is now termed **nanotechnology**. It describes the use of **bhasmas** (metal ash) in treating ailments, now known to contain nanoparticles of metals.
-   Decline of traditional Indian chemistry systems happened due to the introduction of Western medicinal systems in the $20^{th}$ century and the influx of foreign products.
-   Modern chemistry began to grow in India in the late $19^{th}$ century with the arrival of European scientists.

## 1.1 IMPORTANCE OF CHEMISTRY

-   Chemistry is a central science, interconnected with other scientific branches.
-   Its principles apply to diverse areas:
    -   Weather patterns
    -   Brain functioning
    -   Computer operation
    -   Chemical industries (fertilizers, alkalis, acids, salts, dyes, polymers, drugs, soaps, detergents, metals, alloys, new materials)
-   **Contributions to National Economy**: Large-scale production of fertilizers, pesticides, and insecticides.
-   **Healthcare**: Provides methods for isolating and synthesizing life-saving drugs.
    -   Examples: **Cisplatin** and **Taxol** (cancer therapy), **AZT** (Azidothymidine) for AIDS patients.
-   **Material Science**: Understanding chemical principles allows designing and synthesizing new materials with specific magnetic, electric, and optical properties.
    -   Examples: **Superconducting ceramics**, **conducting polymers**, **optical fibers**.
-   **Industries**: Helps establish industries manufacturing utility goods like acids, alkalis, dyes, polymers, metals, contributing to the economy and employment.
-   **Environmental Impact**: Helped in creating safer alternatives for hazardous substances like **CFCs** (chlorofluorocarbons), which cause ozone depletion.
-   **Future Challenges**: Chemists face challenges in managing greenhouse gases (methane, carbon dioxide), understanding biochemical processes, using enzymes for chemical production, and synthesizing exotic materials.

## 1.2 NATURE OF MATTER

-   **Matter**: Anything that has **mass** and **occupies space**.
    -   Examples: Books, pens, water, air, living beings.

### 1.2.1 States of Matter

-   Matter primarily exists in three physical states: **solid**, **liquid**, and **gas**.
-   **Arrangement of particles** in different states:
    -   **Solids**: Particles are very close, in an orderly arrangement, with limited freedom of movement.
    -   **Liquids**: Particles are close but can move around each other.
    -   **Gases**: Particles are far apart and move easily and rapidly.
-   **Characteristics of each state**:
    -   **Solids**: Have definite volume and definite shape.
    -   **Liquids**: Have definite volume but no definite shape; they take the shape of their container.
    -   **Gases**: Have neither definite volume nor definite shape; they completely fill their container.
-   **Interconversion of states**: These states can change into one another by altering temperature and pressure.
    -   Heating a solid usually turns it into a liquid, and further heating turns the liquid into a gas.
    -   Cooling a gas turns it into a liquid (liquefaction), and further cooling freezes the liquid into a solid.

    -   **Mixtures**

## 1.3 PROPERTIES OF MATTER AND THEIR MEASUREMENT

### 1.3.1 Physical and chemical properties

-   Every substance has unique properties. These are categorized as:
    -   **Physical Properties**: Can be measured or observed without changing the substance's identity or composition.
        -   Examples: **Color**, **odor**, **melting point**, **boiling point**, **density**.
    -   **Chemical Properties**: Require a chemical change to occur during their measurement or observation.
        -   Examples: **Composition**, **combustibility**, **reactivity with acids and bases**, **acidity**, **basicity**.
-   Chemists use knowledge of these properties to describe, interpret, and predict substance behavior, determined through careful measurement and experimentation.

### 1.3.2 Measurement of physical properties

-   Scientific investigations require **quantitative measurement** of properties.
-   Quantitative measurements are represented by a number followed by units (e.g., length of a room = 6 m, where 6 is the number and 'm' is the unit).
-   Historically, two systems were used:
    -   **English System**
    -   **Metric System**: Originated in France ($18^{th}$ century), more convenient due to its decimal basis.
-   A common standard system was needed by the scientific community.

### 1.3.3 The International System of Units (SI)

-   The **International System of Units** (Le Système International d'Unités, abbreviated as **SI**) was established in 1960 by the $11^{th}$ General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM).
-   CGPM is an intergovernmental organization formed by the **Metre Convention** (signed in Paris in 1875).
-   The SI system has **seven base units** that correspond to seven fundamental scientific quantities.
-   Other physical quantities (e.g., speed, volume, density) are derived from these base units.

**SI Base Quantities and Units**:

| Base Physical Quantity | Symbol for Quantity | Name of SI Unit | Symbol for SI Unit |
|:-----------------------|:--------------------|:----------------|:-------------------|
| Length                 | $l$                 | metre           | m                  |
| Mass                   | $m$                 | kilogram        | kg                 |
| Time                   | $t$                 | second          | s                  |
| Electric current       | $I$                 | ampere          | A                  |
| Thermodynamic temperature | $T$            | kelvin          | K                  |
| Amount of substance    | $n$                 | mole            | mol                |
| Luminous intensity     | $I_v$               | candela         | cd                 |

-   The SI system uses **prefixes** to denote multiples or submultiples of units (e.g., kilo-, milli-, micro-).

**Maintaining National Standards of Measurement**:
-   Unit definitions are continuously updated as measurement accuracy improves.
-   Each industrial country, including India, has a **National Metrology Institute (NMI)**.
-   In India, this responsibility is with the **National Physical Laboratory (NPL)**, New Delhi.
-   NPL establishes experiments to realize and maintain national standards for base and derived units. These standards are regularly compared with international standards.

### 1.3.4 Mass and Weight

-   **Mass**: The amount of matter present in a substance. It is a constant value.
-   **Weight**: The force exerted by gravity on an object. It can vary depending on gravity (e.g., different on Earth vs. Moon).
-   Mass can be accurately determined using an **analytical balance** in a laboratory.
-   **SI unit of mass**: **Kilogram** (kg).
-   **Common laboratory unit**: **Gram** (g), since smaller amounts of chemicals are used.
    -   Relationship: $1~kg = 1000~g$

### 1.3.5 Volume

-   **Volume**: The amount of space occupied by a substance.
-   **Units of volume**: Derived from length, so it has units of (length)$^3$.
-   **SI unit of volume**: $m^3$ (cubic meter).
-   **Common laboratory units**: For smaller volumes, $cm^3$ or $dm^3$ are used.
-   **Litre (L)**: A common unit for measuring liquid volumes, though not an SI unit.
    -   Relationships:
        -   $1~L = 1000~mL$
        -   $1000~cm^3 = 1~dm^3$
        -   $1~cm^3 = 1~mL$
-   **Measuring devices in the lab**:
    -   Graduated cylinder
    -   Burette
    -   Pipette
    -   Volumetric flask (used to prepare known volumes of solutions)

### 1.3.6 Density

-   **Density**: The amount of mass per unit volume of a substance.
-   **Formula**:
    $$Density = \frac{Mass}{Volume}$$
-   **SI unit of density**: Derived from SI units of mass (kg) and volume ($m^3$).
    $$SI~unit~of~density = \frac{kg}{m^3}~or~kg~m^{-3}$$
-   **Common laboratory unit**: $g~cm^{-3}$ (grams per cubic centimeter) due to smaller masses and volumes.
-   **Concept**: A higher density means the particles of a substance are more closely packed.

### 1.3.7 Temperature

-   **Temperature**: A measure of the hotness or coldness of an object.
-   **Common scales for measurement**:
    -   **Degree Celsius ($^{\circ}C$)**
        -   Freezing point of water: $0^{\circ}C$
        -   Boiling point of water: $100^{\circ}C$
    -   **Degree Fahrenheit ($^{\circ}F$)**
        -   Freezing point of water: $32^{\circ}F$
        -   Boiling point of water: $212^{\circ}F$
    -   **Kelvin (K)**: The **SI unit of temperature**.
        -   Negative temperatures are not possible on the Kelvin scale.
-   **Temperature conversion formulas**:
    -   Celsius to Fahrenheit:
        $$^{\circ}F = \frac{9}{5}(^{\circ}C) + 32$$
    -   Celsius to Kelvin:
        $$K = ^{\circ}C + 273.15$$

## 1.4 UNCERTAINTY IN MEASUREMENT

-   In chemistry, experimental data and theoretical calculations always involve some degree of uncertainty.
-   It's important to handle numbers conveniently and represent data realistically.

### 1.4.1 Scientific Notation

-   Chemists deal with extremely large (e.g., number of molecules) and extremely small (e.g., mass of an atom) numbers.
    -   Example: Mass of a H atom = $0.00000000000000000000000166~g$
    -   Example: Avogadro's number = $602,200,000,000,000,000,000,000$
-   Writing and performing calculations with such numbers can be challenging.
-   **Scientific notation** (or exponential notation) simplifies this by representing any number in the form:
    $$N \times 10^n$$
    -   **N**: A number (digit term) between 1.000... and 9.999...
    -   **n**: An exponent, which can be a positive or negative integer.

**Rounding Off Numbers**:
-   When limiting a result to a required number of significant figures, follow these rules:
    1.  If the rightmost digit to be removed is **more than 5**, increase the preceding number by one.
        -   Example: Rounding 1.386 by removing 6 results in 1.39.
    2.  If the rightmost digit to be removed is **less than 5**, the preceding number is not changed.
        -   Example: Rounding 4.334 by removing 4 results in 4.33.
    3.  If the rightmost digit to be removed is **exactly 5**:
        -   If the preceding number is **even**, it remains unchanged.
        -   If the preceding number is **odd**, it is increased by one.
        -   Example: If 6.35 is to be rounded to two significant figures, it becomes 6.4. If 6.25 is to be rounded, it remains 6.2.

## 1.5 Laws of Chemical Combinations

The combination of elements to form compounds is governed by five basic laws.

### 1.5.1 Law of Conservation of Mass

-   Proposed by **Antoine Lavoisier** in 1789.
-   **Statement**: In all physical and chemical changes, there is no net change in mass during the process. Matter can neither be created nor destroyed.
-   This law was based on careful experimental studies of combustion reactions and precise measurements of reactants and products.
-   It formed a crucial basis for future developments in chemistry.

### 1.5.2 Law of Definite Proportions

-   Given by French chemist **Joseph Proust**.
-   **Statement**: A given compound always contains exactly the same proportion of elements by weight (or mass), irrespective of its source or method of preparation.
-   **Example**: Proust studied cupric carbonate, finding the same percentage composition of copper, carbon, and oxygen in both natural and synthetic samples.
    -   Natural Sample: Cu (51.35%), C (9.74%), O (38.91%)
    -   Synthetic Sample: Cu (51.35%), C (9.74%), O (38.91%)
-   Also known as the **Law of Definite Composition**.

### 1.5.3 Law of Multiple Proportions

-   Proposed by **Dalton** in 1803.
-   **Statement**: If two elements can combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other element are in the ratio of small whole numbers.
-   **Example**: Hydrogen and oxygen combine to form water ($H_2O$) and hydrogen peroxide ($H_2O_2$).
    -   **Water**: 2g Hydrogen + 16g Oxygen $\rightarrow$ 18g Water
    -   **Hydrogen Peroxide**: 2g Hydrogen + 32g Oxygen $\rightarrow$ 34g Hydrogen Peroxide
    -   Here, with a fixed mass of hydrogen (2g), the masses of oxygen (16g and 32g) are in a simple ratio of $16:32$, or $1:2$.

### 1.5.4 Gay Lussac’s Law of Gaseous Volumes

-   Given by **Gay Lussac** in 1808.
-   **Statement**: When gases combine or are produced in a chemical reaction, they do so in a simple ratio by volume, provided all gases are at the same temperature and and pressure.
-   **Example**: 100 mL of hydrogen combine with 50 mL of oxygen to yield 100 mL of water vapour.
    -   Hydrogen + Oxygen $\rightarrow$ Water
    -   100 mL + 50 mL $\rightarrow$ 100 mL
    -   The volumes of hydrogen and oxygen (100 mL and 50 mL) combine in a simple ratio of $2:1$.
-   This law is considered the law of definite proportions by volume.

### 1.5.5 Avogadro’s Law

-   Proposed by **Avogadro** in 1811.
-   **Statement**: Equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure should contain an equal number of molecules.
-   Avogadro distinguished between atoms and molecules, which was a significant idea for its time.
-   His law helped explain Gay Lussac's Law of Gaseous Volumes, especially when considering hydrogen and oxygen as diatomic molecules ($H_2$ and $O_2$), as recognized today.
-   Despite its correctness, Avogadro's proposal initially lacked support. Its importance was emphasized later by Stanislao Cannizaro at the first international chemistry conference in Karlsruhe, Germany, in 1860.

## 1.6 Dalton’s Atomic Theory

-   The idea of indivisible particles (atoms) dates back to Greek philosopher **Democritus** (460–370 BC).
-   **John Dalton** published "A New System of Chemical Philosophy" in 1808, proposing his atomic theory based on experimental studies and the laws of chemical combination.
-   **Postulates of Dalton's Atomic Theory**:
    1.  Matter consists of **indivisible atoms**.
    2.  All atoms of a given element are **identical** in properties, including mass. Atoms of different elements have different masses.
    3.  Compounds are formed when atoms of different elements combine in a **fixed ratio**.
    4.  Chemical reactions involve the **reorganization of atoms**. Atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.
-   **Strengths**: Dalton's theory successfully explained the laws of chemical combination (Conservation of Mass, Definite Proportions, Multiple Proportions).
-   **Limitations**: It could not explain the laws of gaseous volumes or why atoms combine. These were addressed by later scientists.

## 1.7 Atomic and Molecular Masses

### 1.7.1 Atomic Mass

-   The mass of an atom is extremely small.
-   Modern techniques like **mass spectrometry** can determine atomic masses very accurately.
-   Historically, scientists determined relative atomic masses. Hydrogen was initially assigned a mass of 1.
-   **Current System (since 1961)**: Based on **Carbon-12 ($^{12}C$) isotope** as the standard.
    -   $^{12}C$ is assigned a mass of exactly **12 atomic mass units (amu)**.
    -   Masses of all other atoms are given relative to this standard.
-   **One atomic mass unit (amu)**: Defined as a mass exactly equal to one-twelfth ($1/12^{th}$) of the mass of one carbon-12 atom.
    -   $1~amu = 1.66056 \times 10^{-24}~g$
-   Example: Mass of a hydrogen atom $= 1.6736 \times 10^{-24}~g = 1.0080~amu$
-   **Unified mass (u)**: The modern replacement for 'amu'.

### 1.7.2 Average Atomic Mass

-   Many naturally occurring elements exist as **isotopes** (atoms of the same element with different masses due to varying numbers of neutrons).
-   The atomic masses used in calculations and on the periodic table are usually **average atomic masses**.
-   **Calculation**: Average atomic mass is computed by considering the mass of each isotope and its **relative abundance** (percentage occurrence).
-   **Example: Average atomic mass of Carbon**:

| Isotope  | Relative Abundance (%) | Atomic Mass (amu) |
|:---------|:-----------------------|:------------------|
| $^{12}C$ | 98.892                 | 12                |
| $^{13}C$ | 1.108                  | 13.00335          |
| $^{14}C$ | $2 \times 10^{-10}$      | 14.00317          |

Average atomic mass of Carbon = $(0.98892 \times 12~u) + (0.01108 \times 13.00335~u) + (2 \times 10^{-12} \times 14.00317~u) = 12.011~u$

### 1.7.3 Molecular Mass

-   **Molecular mass**: The sum of the atomic masses of all the elements present in a molecule.
-   **Calculation**: Multiply the atomic mass of each element by the number of its atoms in the molecule and then add these values together.
-   **Example: Molecular mass of Methane ($CH_4$)**:
    -   Contains one Carbon atom and four Hydrogen atoms.
    -   Molecular mass of $CH_4 = (1 \times \text{atomic mass of C}) + (4 \times \text{atomic mass of H})$
    -   Molecular mass of $CH_4 = (12.011~u) + (4 \times 1.008~u) = 16.043~u$
-   **Example: Molecular mass of Water ($H_2O$)**:
    -   Contains two Hydrogen atoms and one Oxygen atom.
    -   Molecular mass of $H_2O = (2 \times \text{atomic mass of H}) + (1 \times \text{atomic mass of O})$
    -   Molecular mass of $H_2O = (2 \times 1.008~u) + (16.00~u) = 18.02~u$

### 1.7.4 Formula Mass

-   Used for substances that do not exist as discrete molecules (e.g., ionic compounds like **sodium chloride (NaCl)**).
-   In such compounds, positive ions ($Na^+$) and negative ions ($Cl^-$) are arranged in a continuous three-dimensional structure (crystal lattice).
-   **Formula mass**: Calculated in the same way as molecular mass, by summing the atomic masses of all atoms in the empirical formula of the compound.
-   It represents the mass of one formula unit of the compound.

## Summary

-   Chemistry is the study of matter's composition, structure, properties, and interactions.
-   Matter exists in three states: solid, liquid, and gas, each with distinct particle arrangements and properties.
-   Matter can be classified into elements, compounds, or mixtures.
    -   **Elements** contain only one type of atom.
    -   **Compounds** are formed from elements combining in fixed ratios.
    -   **Mixtures** contain multiple substances and can be homogeneous or heterogeneous.
-   Properties of matter can be physical (observed without chemical change) or chemical (observed through chemical change).
-   Measurements in chemistry require quantitative analysis using **SI units** (International System of Units).
    -   The SI system has seven base units for fundamental quantities (length, mass, time, electric current, temperature, amount of substance, luminous intensity).
-   **Scientific notation** is used to handle very large or very small numbers.
-   **Significant figures** and **rounding rules** ensure data is presented with appropriate certainty.
-   Chemical combinations are governed by fundamental laws:
    -   **Law of Conservation of Mass** (Lavoisier): Mass is neither created nor destroyed.
    -   **Law of Definite Proportions** (Proust): Compounds have fixed element proportions by mass.
    -   **Law of Multiple Proportions** (Dalton): Elements combining to form multiple compounds show simple whole-number ratios of masses.
    -   **Gay Lussac’s Law of Gaseous Volumes**: Gases combine in simple volume ratios at constant temperature and pressure.
    -   **Avogadro’s Law**: Equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules.
-   **Dalton's Atomic Theory** states that atoms are the fundamental building blocks of matter.
-   **Atomic mass** is expressed relative to the $^{12}C$ isotope (12u). **Average atomic mass** accounts for natural isotope abundance.
-   **Molecular mass** is the sum of atomic masses in a molecule. **Formula mass** is used for ionic compounds.
-   The **mole** is a unit representing $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles (Avogadro constant).
-   **Stoichiometry** involves quantitative study of reactants and products in chemical reactions using balanced equations.
-   Solution concentrations can be expressed in various ways like mass percent, mole fraction, molarity, and molality.
